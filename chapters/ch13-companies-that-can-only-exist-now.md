## The Station Agent's Watches

In the mid-1880s, a young man named Richard Sears worked as the station agent for the Minneapolis and St. Louis Railway at Redwood Falls, Minnesota. A station agent's job was to sell tickets, handle freight and keep the telegraph ticking.

Then a shipment of watches arrived for a local jeweler, and the jeweler refused it. Sears could have sent the box back. Instead he did something that would have made no sense twenty years earlier. He wrote to other station agents up and down the line and offered them the watches at a low price. They bought. He made roughly \$5,000 on the deal, and in 1886 he used it to start a mail-order watch business in Minneapolis. The next year he put out a catalog of watches, diamonds and jewelry, and he backed every item with a promise: if you don't like it, send it back and get your money.

He hired a watchmaker named Alvah C. Roebuck. The firm became Sears, Roebuck and Company, and in 1893 it moved to Chicago.

Look at what Sears actually had. He didn't own a factory. He didn't invent the watch or the railroad. What he had was a seat at the exact point where cheap muscle had changed the arithmetic of distance. Steam had made it cheap to move a small box hundreds of miles. Once that was true, a man with a list of customers and a postage stamp could sell to people he would never meet. The jeweler in Redwood Falls saw a refused shipment. Sears saw a business that could only exist now.

This chapter is about that kind of seeing. Earlier chapters covered the first two waves from Chapter 1: buying the tools, then redesigning the work. This one is about the third wave, the businesses that cheap cognition makes possible for the first time. Most leaders walk past it because, like the jeweler, they're looking at the box and not the railroad.

## What Cheap Muscle Really Built

We usually tell the Industrial Revolution as a story of machines: steam engines, locomotives, looms, dynamos. But read the history of the businesses that grew rich on cheap muscle and something odd stands out. The machines were necessary. They were almost never the invention that made the money.

Start with the railroads themselves. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830 as the first inter-city line to run on steam alone. Within a generation, American railroads were the largest businesses the world had ever seen, with thousands of employees spread over hundreds of miles. Nobody had ever managed anything like that. So in 1855, Daniel McCallum, general superintendent of the New York and Erie Railroad, drew a chart showing who reported to whom, who decided what, and how information flowed up and orders flowed down. It was one of the first organization charts in American business. The business historian Alfred Chandler, who studied this period closely, wrote that "McCallum's principles and procedures of management, like his organization chart, were new in American business." The railroad didn't just need engines. It needed a new kind of management to run them.

It needed a new kind of time, too. Before the 1880s, every town kept its own time by the sun, and a railroad crossing a state might juggle dozens of local clocks. On November 18, 1883, the railroads of the United States and Canada simply adopted standard time zones on their own, a day people later called the day of two noons. Cheap muscle had made distance small enough that the clock itself had to change.

Then came retail. In Paris, Aristide Boucicaut became a partner in a shop called Le Bon Marché in 1852 and helped turn it into one of the first great department stores. Its innovations weren't mechanical. Prices were fixed and marked, so nobody haggled. Customers could exchange goods or get refunds. The store carried a far wider range than any small shop could, and even offered a reading room. These were promises, and they worked because steady, cheap supply let a store keep them.

In 1872, Aaron Montgomery Ward mailed a single sheet listing 163 products to rural customers who had no good store nearby. By 1875 his firm promised "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back." The guarantee mattered as much as the catalog, because a farmer in Kansas couldn't inspect a stove he'd never seen. The model grew again when rural free delivery reached farm families at the end of the 1890s, and again when parcel post began in 1913. In 1916, in Memphis, Clarence Saunders opened Piggly Wiggly, the first true self-service grocery, where shoppers walked the aisles and picked goods off the shelves themselves instead of handing a list to a clerk.

Here is the reveal, and it's the thread that ties this whole chapter together. Look at the list again: an organization chart, a time zone, a fixed price, a money-back guarantee, a catalog, a self-service aisle. Almost none of these is a machine. They are **promises and arrangements**: ways of organising work and trust that only became possible, or only became profitable, once moving and making things got cheap.

Sears didn't get rich because steam carried his watches. Steam carried everyone's watches. He got rich because cheap transport let him make a promise no small-town jeweler could match: any watch, any farm, money back if you don't like it. The railroad lowered the cost of keeping that promise until keeping it was a business.

That's the lens for the third wave. Cheap cognition will not make its fortunes by doing old thinking a little faster. It will make them by letting companies keep promises that were too expensive to keep before.

## The List You Couldn't Afford

Every company carries a list it doesn't write down. It's the list of things that would be worth doing if thinking were free.

Call every customer who stopped ordering. Read every contract for the clause that quietly costs you money. Check every invoice line, not a sample. Answer every lost quote with a question about why. Watch every account for the early signs it's about to leave. Nobody objects to any of these. They just don't get done, because each one needs a person's attention, and a person's attention costs about a loaded hour at a time. At Brightwater that hour costs \$42.87. A task worth \$15 a time never clears that bar, so it falls off the bottom of the list and stays there for decades.

Brightwater had already met one of these without noticing what it was. Back in Chapter 2, part of the time freed by the quote Skill went to calling lapsed small customers, a job that had sat on Tom Okafor's wish list for years. Nobody had ever said "we won't call lapsed customers." The calls simply never paid for the hour they took. When the hour came free, they paid.

Now push that one step further. What happens to that list when the cost of attention falls, not by half, but to a few dollars of machine time plus a person's glance?

Everything between the two lines in Figure 13.2 changes category. It stops being "nice to have" and becomes "worth doing." And the band is not thin. In most companies it holds more work than everything above the old line, because there are always far more small decisions than big ones.

Here's a smaller reveal, and it surprises people who expect AI to shrink the amount of thinking work in the world. In 1865, the economist William Stanley Jevons published *The Coal Question*. He noticed that as steam engines grew more efficient at using coal, Britain burned more coal, not less. Cheaper work per unit opened up so many new uses that total demand rose. The same thing tends to happen with any input that suddenly gets cheap, and thinking is no exception. When reading a contract costs a few dollars instead of an hour, companies don't read the same number of contracts more cheaply. They read all of them.

So the right question is not "which tasks can AI do more cheaply?" That question leads straight back to the first wave, and to a slide full of hours that never reach the P&L. The right question is: **what's on the list we could never afford, and who would pay us to do it?**

Some of the answers are internal. Brightwater can now check every invoice line for pricing errors before it goes out, and that lands in the ledger as a Fix line: fewer disputes, less rework. Some of the answers point outward, and those are the interesting ones. If Brightwater can check every invoice line for its own customers, it can probably check every invoice line for somebody else's. A job it could never afford to do for itself is suddenly a job it can afford to do for a fee.

That is how a cost center turns into a product line, and why the skipped list is where freed hours can earn the most.

## Stop Selling Hours

Before we look at what to sell, we need to look at how to charge for it, because one pricing habit quietly destroys most of the value of cheap cognition.

Picture a firm that bills by the hour. It might be a consultancy, an accounting practice, a law office, an agency, or a distributor's in-house service team charging customers for technical support. A piece of work takes an expert ten hours, and the client pays \$200 an hour. Revenue: \$2,000.

Now the firm builds a Skill that does most of the reading and drafting, and the same work takes two hours of expert time. Same expert, same quality, same client. If the firm keeps billing by the hour, revenue on that job falls to \$400. The firm has just done the best work of its year and given itself an 80 percent pay cut.

Figure 13.3: Billing by the hour turns every efficiency gain into lost revenue. Pricing the outcome splits the gain between the customer and the firm.

That's why so many firms that sell time are nervous about AI, and why some quietly use it while billing as if they didn't. That doesn't last. Clients learn what the work really takes.

The way out is old. Companies that sold expensive machines worked it out decades ago.

When Xerox brought out its 914 copier in 1959, the machine was too costly for most offices to buy outright. So Xerox rented it out and charged by the copy, using a meter inside the machine to count. The customer didn't pay for a machine. It paid for copies, the thing it actually wanted. The more useful the machine became, the more copies people made, and the more Xerox earned.

Rolls-Royce went further. In 1962 it introduced a support arrangement for the Viper engine on a small business jet, made by de Havilland and later Hawker Siddeley, that came to be called Power-by-the-Hour. The operator paid a fixed amount for every hour the engine flew. Keeping the engine running became largely Rolls-Royce's problem. That changed who wanted what. Under the old model, the maker earned more when engines broke, because it sold the parts. Under the new one, it earned more when engines stayed in the air. The maker and the customer finally wanted the same thing.

Both companies saw the same truth. When your cost of delivering a result falls, or when you control that cost better than your customer can, you stop selling inputs and start selling the result. Cognition is now reaching exactly that point.

**When thinking costs almost nothing per unit, you sell the outcome, not the hour.**

Go back to the firm in Figure 13.3. Instead of billing two hours, it sells the finished piece of work for \$1,500. The client pays \$500 less than before and is happy. The firm earns \$750 for each hour its expert spent, almost four times its old rate. And the efficiency gain, rather than leaking away, is now split between the two parties in plain sight. Every improvement the firm makes to its Skills from here on raises its margin instead of cutting its revenue.

Outcome pricing comes with a catch, and it's the second smaller reveal of this chapter. When you sell hours, your customer carries the risk of the work going wrong: if the analysis takes longer, they pay more; if it's wrong, they pay you to fix it. When you sell outcomes, that risk moves to you. Every error is now your cost. The error rate you measured in Chapter 12, the compounded yield of a whole stream, stops being a quality statistic and becomes a line in your price.

That's why you price an outcome from the bottom up, and why the ledger you've built is the best pricing tool you own. Here's how Brightwater might price one dispute triaged for another distributor, using numbers it already trusts.

Figure 13.4: The floor is your full cost per correct result, errors included. The ceiling is what the result costs the customer today. The price lives in between.

At the bottom sits the machine's cost per item, a couple of dollars. On top of it goes the expert time that remains: a quarter of an hour of a credit analyst reviewing the agent's work, \$10.72 at Brightwater's loaded rate. Then the cost of the errors you now carry. Brightwater's dispute triage error rate fell from 6 percent to 2 percent, and each error costs about \$310 to unwind, so the errors add \$6.20 to every dispute. Together, those three layers are the **cost per correct outcome**, the stream metric from Chapter 12, and they set the floor. Below it, you lose money on every unit.

The ceiling is what the work costs the customer today. A distributor without the agent spends an analyst hour on each dispute and carries the old 6 percent error rate: \$42.87 plus \$18.60, or \$61.47. Price at \$40 and the customer saves \$21.47 on every dispute, while Brightwater makes \$21.08. Both sides can see exactly why the deal is fair.

One warning, because it's where outcome pricing usually breaks. You must define the outcome so that both sides can count it the same way. "Disputes triaged" is countable. "Better cash flow" is not, at least not without an argument every month. Sell what you can both put on a meter.

## Three Models to Test

Once you stop selling hours, three business models open up. None is new in principle. What's new is that cheap cognition makes them work for companies that could never have run them before.

:::{figure} ../images/ch13-three-models.png
:label: fig-ch13-three-models
:alt: Three columns for outcome pricing, productised service, and selling your own Skills and agents, each showing how it pays, what Brightwater could do with it, and the main thing to watch out for
:width: 100%
:align: center

Figure 13.5: Three ways to sell what your machines and your experts produce together, each with a Brightwater example and the trap that comes with it.
:::

**Outcome pricing** sells a countable result. You've just seen it with disputes. Brightwater's version could be bolder: offer customers an audit of their own incoming invoices, paid as a share of the overcharges it recovers. A few years ago that service would have needed a room full of auditors to break even. With an agent reading every line and a specialist checking the flagged ones, the cost per invoice is small enough that a share of the recoveries is a real business. The trap is definition. If the customer and Brightwater count "recovered" differently, every invoice becomes a debate.

**A productised service** sells a fixed package of work for a fixed price, usually monthly: one scope, one price, delivered by a stream of Skills and agents with people at the checkpoints. Brightwater belongs to a buying group of independent distributors, most of them far smaller than Brightwater. Many of them struggle with order entry: purchase orders arrive as emails, PDFs and phone calls, and a clerk keys every one. Brightwater has already built the stream that handles this, from PO intake through credit to confirmation. It could offer to run order entry for the smaller members for a fixed monthly fee per account. The trap here is scope creep. Each small exception you agree to handle by hand drags the service back toward selling hours. Productised services stay profitable only when the scope is written down and the exceptions are priced separately.

**Selling your own Skills and agents** turns know-how into a product. Remember what a Skill is: packaged expertise, a written recipe that lets the machine do a task the way your best person would. Remember what an agent is: autonomous cognition, a digital worker that handles a whole job. Both are assets. You built them, you tested them against real work, and you know their error rates. Brightwater's dispute triage agent carries years of Lena's judgment about which disputes are real and which are pricing confusion. Other distributors would pay for that, either as a license they run themselves or as a subscription Brightwater runs for them.

This is the model that would have been unthinkable in the age of muscle. A railroad couldn't sell its management know-how in a box. A department store couldn't ship its best buyer's eye to a rival in another city. But cognition, once written down as a Skill, can be copied at almost no cost. Expertise that used to live in one person's head, and walked out the door when they retired, can now be packaged, tested, versioned and sold. Whatever the tools are called in ten years, that fact won't change: codified judgment is a product.

The trap is the sharpest of the three. Never sell the thing that is your edge. If Brightwater's quote Skill is the reason it wins business against larger rivals, licensing it to those rivals is giving away the store. Sell the Skills that solve common problems well, the ones every distributor needs and nobody competes on. Keep the ones that make customers choose you.

Here is how the three compare on the questions leaders ask first.

| Model | What the customer buys | What you must be able to count | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome pricing | A result, paid per result | Results both sides agree on, and your cost per correct result | A vague outcome turns every bill into a dispute |
| Productised service | A fixed package for a fixed price | The work inside the scope, and every exception outside it | Unpriced exceptions drag it back to selling hours |
| Your Skills and agents | Your know-how, licensed or run for them | Accuracy, usage and support cost per customer | Selling the very thing that makes you win |

Notice what all three need: a measured cost per correct outcome, a known error rate and a clear owner. That's what the earlier chapters of this book have been building. A company that never mapped its streams, never measured its error rates and never put its freed hours on a ledger has no idea what an outcome costs it, and can't price one without guessing. The third wave is built on the second.

## The Floor Plan, Again

In Chapter 1, the factory owners who got rich from electricity weren't the ones who bought the biggest motors. They were the ones who redrew the floor around small motors on every machine. The redrawn floor plan was the second wave. Then, on top of the new floors and the new railroads, came the third: businesses like Sears, built around promises that cheap muscle made affordable.

The same order holds now. You cannot sell an outcome you can't deliver reliably. You can't run a productised service on a stream that still waits two days at every handoff. You can't license a Skill whose error rate you've never measured. That's why this chapter comes last among the leadership chapters, not first. Third-wave businesses are built from second-wave parts.

But the third wave doesn't need a separate department, a moonshot budget or a strategy retreat. It needs the same discipline you've used all along. Treat each new model as a ticket on the ledger board from Chapter 10. Give it an owner, a value case and a date. Size it with the same three numbers, theoretical, projected and realized, that you use for every other piece of work. Most new models will start under the **Grow** lever, because they bring in revenue that didn't exist before, and some will be large enough to run as an **Invest** line with a charter of their own.

Then test small. Dana Ruiz didn't stake Brightwater on order entry for the whole buying group. She asked Priya Shah to sign two small distributors for a ninety-day pilot, at a fixed monthly fee, with Sam Whitfield measuring cost per correct order every week and Marcus Bell watching the margin. If the stream holds its accuracy on someone else's messy purchase orders, the pilot moves across the board. If it doesn't, it gets parked or killed, like any other ticket, and the company learns something cheap.

A new business model sounds like a bet. Run through the ledger, it becomes a series of small, measured experiments, each of which earns its way across the board or doesn't.

Richard Sears didn't plan a retail empire at Redwood Falls. He sold one refused box of watches to people he'd never met, noticed it worked, and did it again with a catalog and a promise. Somewhere on your own skipped list is a box of watches. The railroad has already arrived.

## Monday Morning

1. Write your company's too-expensive list: ten things that would be worth doing if thinking cost a few dollars a time instead of an hour.
2. Find every place you still bill by the hour, and work out what a 50 percent efficiency gain would do to that revenue.
3. Pick one outcome you could sell, and price it from the bottom up: machine cost, remaining expert time, errors you'd carry, then margin, under the customer's current cost.
4. List the Skills and agents you've already built, and mark which ones are your edge and which ones solve common problems.
5. Put one new model on the ledger board as a ticket with an owner, a ninety-day pilot and a cost-per-correct-outcome target.

*The fortunes of the age of muscle went to those who used cheap power to keep new promises, and the fortunes of the age of mind will go to those who sell the outcome that cheap thinking finally makes affordable.*
