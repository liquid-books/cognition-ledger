# Chapter 9: Prove: It Isn't Savings Until Finance Says So

*The only savings number is the one the CFO will sign.*

The night before the board meeting, Dana Ruiz had two pages on the desk, and they disagreed by almost a million dollars.

The first came from the team's AI tools, the kind of usage dashboard every vendor provides. It was handsome. It said Brightwater's Skills and agents had delivered *\$1.2 million in value* in their first year. It had a green arrow pointing up and to the right.

The second came from Marcus Bell, and it was a single sheet with one number circled in pencil: **\$288,463**.

"That's less than a quarter of the other one," Dana said.

"It's the only number on either page I'll sign," Marcus said. "The \$1.2 million is hours times a rate. It's real hours, and I believe them. But hours aren't on my P&L. The \$288,463 is five lines I can show the board, with the document behind every dollar."

Dana had sat through a lot of board meetings, long enough to know what a member of an audit committee does with a big round number: they ask how you know. Dana took Marcus's page.

The next morning the question came, right on cue, from the chair of the audit committee. "How do you know?" Marcus walked the board through all five lines in about four minutes: the four requisitions struck from the headcount plan, the two temp contracts ended, the overtime report against the same months last year, the dispute contractor not renewed, the credit-memo totals against baseline. Then Marcus put up the part nobody expected a finance chief to volunteer: the 9,401 freed hours that still had no destination, and the two automations running with no plan for their hours at all.

The room went quiet in the good way. The chair said, "This is the first AI update I've understood." The board asked to see the same page every quarter.

This chapter is about that page: what goes on it, what never goes on it, and why the smaller number is the one that wins.

**The only savings number is the one the CFO will sign.**

## Three Numbers That Never Touch

When you sell a house, three numbers matter. The asking price is what you hope for. The accepted offer is what a buyer has agreed to pay, on conditions. The closing price is what lands in your account. All three are real numbers. All three are useful. And no estate agent in history has added them together and announced the total as "sales this month". The agent who did would be fired by lunchtime.

The Cognition Ledger has the same three numbers, and it keeps them just as far apart.

**Theoretical value** is the asking price: net hours freed times the loaded hourly rate. It tells you the size of the opportunity. For Brightwater's eight use cases, three quarters in, it's \$1,214,780 a year.

**Projected banked value** is the accepted offer: value with a lever, a P&L line, a budget owner and a date in a signed Hour Budget. It's what the plan intends to land. For Brightwater, \$1,292,292 a year.

**Realized value** is the closing price: money the CFO, or a finance partner the CFO names, has verified against evidence. It's the only number that counts as savings. For Brightwater, \$288,463 so far.

:width: 100%

Figure 9.1: Three numbers, three meanings. Report all three every quarter, and never add, net or blend them.

Look at the first two and you'll see why they can never be added, or even treated as steps on one staircase. Projected banked value is *larger* than theoretical value. That's not an error. Chapter 8 showed why: Fix banks errors that no longer happen and Speed banks cash that arrives sooner, and neither comes from hours. The numbers measure different things. Adding them is like adding a house's asking price to its square footage.

The house analogy breaks in one useful place. A house closes once, and the money stays closed. Savings don't. A requisition struck from the plan can be quietly reinstated next spring. An overtime habit can creep back. That's why realized value is verified again every quarter, not once, and why a line that was verified last year can lose its status this year.

Each number also has its own audience, which is another reason to keep them apart. Theoretical value is for the people choosing what to build: it sizes the prize and ranks the ideas. Projected banked value is for the budget owners: it's the plan they signed, and it tells them what they've promised to deliver and by when. Realized value is for the board, the auditors and anyone outside the room: it's the only number that has been checked by someone with nothing to gain from it. Blend them and every audience gets the wrong number. The builders stop sizing honestly, the budget owners lose track of their promises, and the board hears a figure that collapses at the first good question.

There's a practical rule that follows. Whenever a number about AI value leaves the room, in a deck, an email or an annual report, it travels with its label. "Theoretical" and "projected" are not embarrassing words. They're the words that keep the third number believable.

## Two Ratios That Tell You Where to Look

Three numbers become a management tool when you compare them. Two ratios do the work.

The **realization rate** is realized value divided by projected banked value. It asks: of what we planned to land, how much has finance verified? For Brightwater, \$288,463 ÷ \$1,292,292 = **22.3%**.

The **realization gap** is theoretical value minus realized value. It asks: how much of the original promise hasn't reached the P&L yet? For Brightwater, \$1,214,780 minus \$288,463 = **\$926,317**.

Is 22.3% bad? Here's the first surprise of this chapter: on its own, you can't tell. A realization rate is a speedometer, not a report card, and you have to read it against the calendar.

Most Hour Budget lines are annual, and the money arrives quarter by quarter. Brightwater's Avoid line for the quote builder is planned at \$322,382 a year. After two closed and verified quarters, finance has verified \$161,192 of it, almost exactly half. That line is at 50%, and it's perfectly on schedule. Other lines haven't reached their dates yet. The PO intake agent's two clerks aren't due to leave until the third quarter. The dispute agent's DSO line and its retiring analyst both start in the third quarter. The credit memo and collections Fix lines are dated for the fourth. Those lines sit at zero because their quarter hasn't come, not because anyone failed.

So Brightwater reads the rate the way a pilot reads a fuel gauge: against where it should be by now. The framework's alarm bell is simple. **Below 50% a year after go-live means the Hour Budget isn't being worked.** At three quarters in, 22.3% with most lines on schedule is a healthy start. At twelve months, it would be a fire.

Break the rate down by lever and a second pattern appears, one that holds in almost every organization that runs this discipline.

Cut leads at 41% verified, because a canceled contract is the easiest evidence in finance. Avoid follows at 31%, because a struck requisition is nearly as clean. Fix is at 13%, because error reports take a full quarter of history to compare. Grow and Speed are at zero: Grow because it needs a control group, Speed because its date hasn't come. Hard evidence realizes first. That should shape which levers you lean on early, when you need the program to earn trust.

A word of caution about the ratio itself. Once people know they'll be judged on realization rate, the easiest way to raise it is to promise less. That's Goodhart's law, in the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's well-known phrasing: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Brightwater guards against it by never reporting the rate alone. The rate sits beside the gap and the leak, so a team that sandbagged its plan would show a lovely rate and an ugly pile of unassigned hours.

## The Verification Protocol

Realized value needs a referee. The verification protocol is the rulebook, and it has five rules, each one short enough to put on a sticky note.

**Finance names one partner.** At Brightwater it's the Controller, who verifies ledger lines every quarter. Not the project team, not the vendor, not the value stream owner. The person who verifies can't be the person who benefits from the number.

**Each lever has an evidence standard.** They're the evidence column of Chapter 8's lever table: a canceled contract for Cut, a struck requisition for Avoid, sales against a control group for Grow, an error or write-off report for Fix, a DSO report and treasury's cost of capital for Speed. No evidence, no verification.

**Compare the same months a year earlier.** Overtime in December means nothing next to overtime in March. Most businesses have seasons, and comparing a busy quarter to a quiet one manufactures savings out of the calendar.

**Grow needs a control group.** A comparable team or territory that didn't get the extra hours, or a before-and-after against a matched team. Without one, a Grow line stays at projected, however good the story.

**Verified lines get a date.** In the Bank Plan, a line is marked verified with the date finance saw the evidence. Only verified lines count as realized.

None of this is exotic. It's the same discipline finance already applies to revenue, inventory and expenses, pointed at a new kind of claim. At Brightwater it costs the Controller about a day each quarter, because the evidence was named in the Hour Budget before anything was built. When a team has to go looking for proof after the fact, verification takes weeks and usually ends in an argument. When the proof was agreed up front, it takes an afternoon.

Here is the protocol in action, and it produces the chapter's second surprise.

Tom Okafor's team did exactly what their Hour Budget promised. They spent their freed hours calling lapsed customers, and the CRM shows those customers ordering again: \$14,000 of margin in the first quarter and \$19,500 in the second, \$33,500 in all. Tom believes every dollar of it. So does Priya. So, privately, does Marcus.

None of it counts. The control territory comparison hasn't been run yet, so nobody can say how many of those customers would have come back on their own. The line sits at projected, with \$33,500 recorded and \$0 realized, until the comparison is done.

**Brightwater has \$33,500 it believes in and can't count. That's the protocol working, not failing.**

Tom was annoyed for about a week. Then Tom saw the other side of it. Once that comparison is done, the team's number will be the one Grow line in the company that nobody can argue with. A believed number gets debated at every budget meeting. A verified number gets funded.

So where did the \$288,463 come from? From exactly five lines, each with its document.

## When the Money Un-Closes

Verification isn't a ceremony you perform once and frame on the wall. Every quarter, the Controller looks at every verified line again, because realized value can slip back out of the books as quietly as it came in.

Imagine next spring. One of Tom's reps leaves, the team has a frantic month, and a branch manager reopens one of the four struck requisitions without telling anyone in finance. Nothing about the quote builder has changed. It still frees the same hours. But a quarter of the Avoid line's evidence has just disappeared, and at the next verification the Controller marks that portion down. The dashboard shows the drop, with a date and a reason.

That's not the program failing. That's the books being true. A ledger that can only go up is a marketing document.

When a line fails verification, or sits at projected quarter after quarter, the value stream owner has three honest moves. **Fix the evidence**: run the control comparison, pull the overtime report, find the canceled contract. **Re-scope the line**: if the hours really went somewhere else, move them to the lever that describes what actually happened, and price it that way. **Mark it down**: return the hours to Leak, valued at \$0, and bring them to the next budget conversation. What nobody gets to do is leave a line at projected forever, hoping no one notices.

**A line that has been "about to verify" for four quarters is Leak wearing a tie.**

The same thinking tells you how to read the realization gap. Brightwater's \$926,317 isn't one problem; it's three, and they need different responses. The biggest and most urgent part is the leak: hours worth \$343,614 at loaded cost with no destination at all, which no amount of patience will turn into money. The next part is lines whose dates haven't arrived, which need nothing but time and a reminder in the right quarter. The last part is money that has happened but can't yet be proved, like Tom's \$33,500, which needs evidence. A good finance partner sorts the gap into those three piles every quarter, and the executive sponsor spends attention in that order: leak first, evidence second, calendar last.

## Five Ways a Savings Number Lies

Most inflated AI numbers aren't fraud. They're honest people making one of five mistakes, and the protocol exists to catch every one.

The first is the most common: **reporting theoretical value as savings**. Hours times a rate goes on a slide with the word "saved" next to it. Chapter 6 called it a promise, not a payment; the dashboard on Dana's desk was this mistake in a nice font.

The second is **counting the same hour twice**. One team claims the freed hours avoided a hire; another claims the same hours generated sales. The Hour Budget's rule (one hour, one destination) catches this before build, and verification catches it after.

The third is **the phantom requisition**: "avoiding" a hire that was never in the approved plan. If the job was never budgeted, not filling it saves nothing. Finance checks the plan as it stood before the AI arrived.

The fourth is **pricing at the wrong rate**. Speed priced at the loaded rate instead of the cost of capital. Grow priced at revenue instead of margin. Or the classic: the one-time \$3.45 million of cash from Chapter 8 booked as if it arrived every year.

The fifth is **letting the season do the work**: comparing a peak quarter's overtime with a quiet quarter's and calling the difference savings. Same months, a year earlier, every time.

Each mistake makes the number bigger, which is why each one is tempting. And each one is found eventually, usually by the person you least want to find it.

## The Best News a Board Can Hear

Here is the page Marcus took into the boardroom, three quarters into the program, with two quarters of books closed and verified.

:::

Read it the way the board did. Eight use cases in the ledger. 32,301 net hours a year freed, after review and oversight time. A theoretical value of \$1,214,780, labeled as the opportunity, not the savings. A projected banked value of \$1,292,292, every dollar of it with a lever, an owner and a date. \$288,463 realized and verified by the Controller. A realization rate of 22.3%, on schedule for the calendar. And \$691,370 a year of cost of poor quality avoided across the portfolio, errors that no longer reach customers or the ledger, of which only the part tied to Fix lines is counted as banked.

The page carries the cost side too, because a board that sees only value is being sold to. Everything in the ledger cost \$256,000 to build and pilot, and costs \$130,000 a year to run: licences, usage, monitoring and support. Those sit on the same page as the verified \$288,463, so the board can do its own arithmetic instead of trusting someone else's.

Then the part that made the audit committee chair sit up. **Leak: 9,401 hours a year, worth \$343,614 at loaded cost, still owed a decision.** Two lines on the page are red. The proof-of-delivery bot (OTC-06), plain workflow automation, is being built with no Hour Budget at all, so all 1,363 of its hours are leaking. The cash application agent (OTC-07) is sized but has no plan for its 4,026 hours, so it isn't allowed into Building until it gets one. A third, the credit memo Skill (OTC-03), is live with only a Fix line banked, so most of its freed time is leaking too.

Now the chapter's big reveal. Most executives assume the board wants the biggest number they can defend. It doesn't. A board has heard a hundred big numbers, and it has learned that big numbers fall apart under one good question. What a board almost never hears is a management team that knows exactly where every hour went, has a quarter of its planned money already verified on the books, and volunteers the list of hours it hasn't yet given a job.

That page says three things no savings claim can say. *We are not fooling ourselves.* *We know what we owe.* *And we will be back next quarter with the same page, so you can check.* It turns AI from a technology story, which boards find hard to judge, into a financial story told the way every other financial story is told: with books, evidence and a named person who signs. A board can fund that. Brightwater's did.

The tools will change. In ten years the dashboard will be generated by something we haven't named yet, and the Skills and agents on it will look primitive. The discipline at the heart of AI Value Stream Mapping won't change, because it isn't about AI. It's about the oldest rule in finance: you don't count money until it's in the account, and you don't trust a number nobody is willing to sign.

## Monday Morning

- Put three numbers on your next AI update, theoretical, projected banked and realized, in separate boxes, and never add them.
- Ask your CFO to name one finance partner who will verify your ledger lines every quarter.
- For each line in your Hour Budget, write down the document that proves it, and the same months last year you'll compare against.
- Find every Grow line and design its control group now, before the sales start arriving.
- Put your leak hours and any automation without an Hour Budget on the page in red, and bring that page to the board.

*AI value becomes savings only when an independent finance partner has seen the evidence, and the honest page that shows it is the one a board will fund.*
