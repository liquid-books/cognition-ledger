Priya Shah walked into Marcus Bell's office with the best slide of a long career.

The quote builder pilot was done. Twenty real quotes, measured with a stopwatch, review time included. The slide said, in big type, *10,246 hours a year freed*, and underneath, *\$439,270*. Priya had rehearsed the sentence in the lift: the Skill pays for its build cost in under a month.

Brightwater's CFO read the slide twice. Then came the question that ends most AI business cases. "Lovely. Which line of my P&L does it go on?"

Priya started to explain loaded rates. Marcus held up a hand, turned the monitor round and opened next year's headcount plan. Inside sales, Tom Okafor's team: four new reps, approved, budgeted, staggered across the year to keep up with growth. Four requisitions, each one a full-time salary plus benefits, sitting in a spreadsheet waiting to be filled.

"If your Skill gives Tom's team the capacity of four reps," Marcus said, "and Tom agrees to absorb the growth without hiring them, I take these four lines out of the plan. That's money I never spend, and I can show you exactly where it went." Marcus tapped the screen. "That's a savings number. The rest of your slide is a hope until somebody tells me where those hours are going."

Priya looked at the four requisitions for a long moment. The money had been there all along, in a plan nobody in Order-to-Cash had ever opened. The AI hadn't created it. It had only made it possible. A *decision* would create it.

That conversation is the hinge of this book. Everything before it (mapping, sorting, sizing, building) produces hours. Everything after it depends on what happens to those hours. This chapter is where time becomes money, and it's where most organizations quietly lose it. It's also the step that most separates CVSM from the classic value stream mapping it grew out of: classic mapping counts the capacity you free, and stops there.

## A Seating Chart for Time

Anyone who has planned a wedding knows the seating chart. Two hundred guests, twenty tables, and every name has to land somewhere. Nobody hands out the chart *after* the guests arrive. You do it weeks before, because you know what happens to a guest without a table: they drift to the bar, and you never quite see them again.

Freed hours behave exactly like that. When a Skill takes twenty minutes off a quote, those twenty minutes don't arrive in a neat pile at the end of the year. They arrive scattered, a few minutes at a time, across 22 people and 660 quotes a week. With no table waiting, they drift: into a longer email, an extra meeting, a little more polish, an earlier finish on Friday. Chapter 2 named the force behind this, Parkinson's law, the observation that work expands to fill the time available. It isn't laziness. It's physics for calendars.

The **Hour Budget** is the seating chart. It's a short plan that gives every freed hour a destination, called a **lever**, and ties each lever to a line of the P&L, a way of pricing it, the evidence finance will accept, a budget owner and a date. Any hour without a lever has a name too: **Leak**. And the ledger values Leak at exactly \$0, however big the theoretical value looks, because nothing on the P&L changes when an hour drifts to the bar.

Where the analogy breaks is the part that should worry you. A guest without a table at least complains. An unassigned hour makes no noise at all. It vanishes into a busy-looking day, and a year later nobody can say where it went, because it never went anywhere you could point to.

That's why the Hour Budget is drafted before anyone builds. Once the Skill ships, the hours start dissolving on day one, and trying to round them up afterwards is like trying to collect the spare change from a thousand pockets. You can't. You have to decide where the money goes before it's in the pockets.

**Decide where the hours go before you build, or they'll leak after you ship.**

## Six Places an Hour Can Go

There are only six tables in the room. Every freed hour, and some value that isn't hours at all, lands at one of them.

| Lever | What happens | P&L line | Evidence the CFO accepts | Priced at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cut** | Stop paying for something: overtime, temps, contractors, an outside service | Operating expense | Canceled contract or PO; overtime report against the same months last year | The rate you stop paying |
| **Avoid** | Absorb growth or attrition without hires the plan called for | Personnel expense against budget | Approved headcount plan with the requisition removed | Loaded rate of the role not hired |
| **Grow** | Move hours to revenue work: selling, retention, new offers | Revenue and gross margin | Sales attributed to the activity, against a control group | Gross margin per redeployed hour |
| **Fix** | Fewer errors, credits, write-offs and penalties | Credits, bad debt, cost of goods, penalties | Error log or write-off report against baseline | Cost of poor quality avoided |
| **Speed** | Shorter lead time turns into cash sooner | Interest expense and working capital | Days sales outstanding or lead-time report | Cash freed times cost of capital |
| **Invest** | Hours go to a funded strategic project | That project's own business case | Project charter with owner, value case, dates | Booked in that project's case |

Each lever is worth a minute of your attention, because each one fails in its own way.

**Cut** is the hardest money there is, and finance's favorite. You stop paying someone outside the company. Brightwater's PO intake agent (OTC-02), which reads emailed purchase orders and keys them into the order system, let the order-entry team end two agency temp contracts, 3,760 hours a year priced at the temps' bill rate, \$133,104. It also ended the month-end overtime crunch: 600 hours at time and a half, \$19,902. The evidence is a canceled purchase order and an overtime report compared with the same months a year earlier. Nobody argues with a canceled contract.

**Avoid** is the lever in Marcus's office. You were going to hire, because of growth or because someone is retiring, and now you don't need to. Finance calls this cost avoidance, and it's real only if the hire was in the approved plan. That catch matters more than it sounds. The most common trick in AI business cases (usually an innocent one) is to "avoid" a hire nobody had approved. If the requisition was never in the budget, removing it saves nothing. Brightwater's rule: show me the requisition, then show me it's gone.

**Grow** moves hours to work that brings in revenue: calling lapsed customers, following up quotes, retention. It's the lever everyone loves to propose, because it sounds like growth instead of cost-cutting. It's also the lever finance trusts least, for good reason. Did the new calls cause the sales, or would the customers have come back anyway? So Grow is priced at gross margin, not revenue (the margin is what the company actually keeps), and it needs a control group, a comparable team or territory that didn't get the extra hours, before finance will believe it.

**Grow is the lever everyone wants and the one finance trusts least.**

**Fix** banks fewer mistakes. Its price is the cost of poor quality avoided, often shortened to COPQ: how many errors no longer happen, times what each error costs to find and fix, including credits, write-offs, penalties and rework. Brightwater's dispute agent cut wrong credits from 6% of disputes to 2%. At 225 disputes a week, 47 working weeks and \$310 per wrong credit, that's 225 × 47 × 0.04 × \$310 = \$131,130 a year. The collections Skill (OTC-05) freed 1,500 hours that the team now spends calling accounts more than 90 days overdue, aiming at \$60,000 a year less in write-offs. Fix lands on lines a CFO already watches.

**Speed** turns shorter lead time into cash. It's strange enough to get its own section below.

**Invest** sends hours to a funded strategic project that has its own business case and owner. The value is real, but it's booked in that project's case, not here, so it never gets counted twice. Brightwater has no Invest lines yet. That's normal early on, and it's healthy: Invest is the lever teams reach for when they can't think of a real destination, so it should require a charter with a name and a date on it.

## The Rules That Keep the Bank Honest

A lever is a promise. Six banking rules keep the promises honest, and each one exists because someone, somewhere, broke it.

**Decide before you build.** A draft Hour Budget is a gate into Building. You saw it bite Sam in Chapter 7.

**The budget owner signs.** The person whose budget line changes signs the Hour Budget, not the project team. Sam can't promise that four reps won't be hired; only the VP who owns inside sales can. A signature from someone who doesn't own the budget is a wish with a pen.

**One hour, one destination.** The hours assigned can't add up to more than the hours freed. The same hour can't avoid a hire *and* make sales calls. It sounds obvious, and it's the most common error in AI business cases. When three teams each claim the same freed hour, the slide looks three times better and the P&L looks exactly the same.

**Name the date.** Every line has a realization date and a quarter in which finance will look for the money. A line with no date is a line nobody will ever check.

**Price it like finance would.** Cut uses the rate you stop paying. Avoid uses the loaded rate of the hire you don't make. Grow uses gross margin. Speed uses the cost of capital, never the loaded rate. This rule produces a small surprise. At Brightwater, an inside-sales hour costs \$42.87 loaded, and an hour of calls to lapsed accounts earns \$35 of gross margin. So moving an hour from quoting to selling is worth *less* on paper than not hiring someone to do that hour. The exciting lever is often the smaller number. That's not an argument against growth; it's an argument for pricing it honestly.

**Show the leak.** Unassigned hours are reported every quarter until someone assigns them or the use case is re-scoped. Leak is never hidden, rounded away or quietly folded into a Grow line.

## Banking OTC-01, Line by Line

Now watch the rules work on the quote builder, from the 10,246 hours of Chapter 6 to a number Marcus would put in front of a board.

The Skill frees 10,246 hours a year after review and price-file upkeep. At a loaded rate of \$42.87 an hour, the theoretical value is \$439,270. Remember Chapter 6's signature line: theoretical value is a promise, not a payment. The Hour Budget is where the promise gets a payment plan.

| Line | Lever | What happens | Hours | Value per year | Evidence | Owner, date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Avoid | Four planned inside-sales hires not made; the current team absorbs growth | 7,520 | \$322,382 | Requisitions removed from the approved plan | VP Inside Sales, Q1 |
| 2 | Grow | Proactive calls to lapsed accounts | 2,000 | \$70,000 | Sales against a control territory | VP Inside Sales, Q2 |
| | Leak | Not yet assigned | 726 | \$0 | | |
| | **Total** | | **10,246** | **\$392,382** | | |

Line 1 is Marcus's four requisitions. A full-time person gives the company about 1,880 productive hours a year (2,080 paid hours less holidays, vacation and sick time), so four reps is 4 × 1,880 = 7,520 hours. Priced at the loaded rate of the role not hired, 7,520 × \$42.87 = \$322,382. The evidence is the simplest document in finance: the headcount plan, with four lines struck out.

Line 2 is Tom Okafor's idea. The reps had a list of customers who used to order and stopped, and never had time to call them. Now they do: 2,000 hours a year, priced at \$35 of gross margin per call hour, \$70,000. It will count only when the sales from those calls can be told apart from a territory that didn't make them.

Then the line most business cases would hide. 726 hours, a little over 15 hours a week spread across 22 reps, less than three-quarters of an hour per person per week. Too thin and scattered to become a hire not made or a block of calls. So it's shown as Leak, valued at \$0, and reported every quarter until someone finds it a seat.

Look at the bottom line: \$392,382 projected banked, against \$439,270 theoretical. The difference, about \$47,000, comes from exactly two honest choices. The 726 leaking hours would have been worth about \$31,000 at the loaded rate and are booked at zero. And the 2,000 Grow hours are priced at \$35 of margin, not \$42.87 of cost, which takes off about another \$16,000. The plan is smaller than the promise because it's priced the way finance prices things.

And even \$392,382 isn't savings yet. It's projected banked value: value with a named lever, a P&L line, a budget owner and a date. Only the Avoid line has hard evidence behind it so far. Chapter 9 is about the day finance checks.

**A smaller number you can defend beats a bigger number you can't.**

## The Twist: Cash From Hours Nobody Saved

So far every dollar has come from an hour. Here's where that stops being true, and it's the idea in this chapter that surprises CFOs most.

Brightwater sells to contractors and plant buyers on credit. When it ships an order, it sends an invoice, and the money arrives weeks later. Finance measures that delay as **days sales outstanding**, or DSO: on average, how many days of sales are sitting in receivables, earned but not yet collected. A disputed invoice is the slowest kind, because nobody pays an invoice they're arguing about.

The dispute triage agent from Chapter 7 gathers the facts and drafts a resolution within four working hours instead of leaving the dispute in a queue. The result: disputes close three days sooner, and Brightwater's DSO falls by three days.

Now do the arithmetic. Brightwater's revenue is \$420 million a year. Divide by 365 and each day of sales is worth about \$1.15 million. Three fewer days of DSO means three days of sales that used to sit in customers' hands are now in Brightwater's bank account:

> \$420M ÷ 365 × 3 = **\$3.45 million of cash**, released once.

What is that cash worth? That depends on the company's **cost of capital**, the yearly rate it pays to borrow money or the return it requires on money it invests. Brightwater's treasury uses 7%. Holding \$3.45 million less in receivables is worth what it would cost to borrow it:

> \$3.45M × 7% = **\$241,644 a year**.

:::{figure} ../images/ch08-speed-twist.png
:label: fig-ch08-speed-twist
:alt: A step-by-step calculation showing 420 million dollars of revenue divided by 365 days, times 3 days of DSO reduction, equals 3.45 million dollars of cash freed, times a 7 percent cost of capital, equals 241,644 dollars a year, with zero hours involved
:width: 100%
:align: center

Figure 8.4: Speed is priced at the cost of capital, never at the loaded rate. The \$3.45 million arrives once; the \$241,644 arrives every year.
:::

Notice what's missing from that calculation: hours. Not one freed hour went into it. The value came from the *wait time* the agent removed, the days a dispute sat in a queue, not from the minutes anybody spent typing. Chapter 3 showed that most office work spends 90% or more of its life waiting. This is what that waiting was costing.

Two honest cautions. The \$3.45 million is a one-time release of working capital, not an annual saving; only the \$241,644 recurs, and only the \$241,644 goes in the Hour Budget. And the cash is worth 7% only if treasury actually puts it to work, paying down a credit line or funding something that would otherwise be borrowed. That's why the Speed line's budget owner is the CFO's own treasury function, and its evidence is the DSO report plus treasury's cost of capital.

Now put the dispute agent's whole Hour Budget side by side with its hours, and the chapter's big reveal appears.

The agent frees 4,383 hours a year. At the analysts' loaded rate, that's a theoretical value of \$175,776. Its Hour Budget banks four lines: an agency dispute specialist's contract not renewed (Cut, \$85,277); a retiring AR analyst not replaced (Avoid, \$75,407); fewer wrong credits (Fix, \$131,130); and three days off DSO (Speed, \$241,644). Projected banked value: **\$533,458**. That's three times what its hours are worth, and the biggest banked number in Brightwater's entire portfolio, from a use case with less than half the hours of the quote builder.

**The most valuable thing AI gives you may not be an hour at all.**

This is why sizing a use case by hours alone can point you at the wrong project. The same thing happens across the whole portfolio: Brightwater's theoretical value is \$1,214,780, and its projected banked value is \$1,292,292. The plan is *larger* than the promise, because Fix banks errors that never happen and Speed banks cash that arrives sooner. If you'd ranked Brightwater's ideas by hours saved, the dispute agent would have been third. Ranked by banked value, it's first.

## The People Inside the Budget

Every Hour Budget is, underneath, a decision about people's jobs. That's why so many AI programs never write one. As long as the hours stay vague, nobody has to say out loud what happens to the people whose work just got shorter.

The Cognition Ledger doesn't make that decision for you, but it won't let it stay vague. Look at the levers Brightwater actually pulled. Four planned hires not made. A retiring analyst not replaced. Two clerks not backfilled when they move on. Two agency temps, a contractor and a month-end overtime crunch ended. Calls to lapsed customers that had been on the wish list for years. Not one person on Brightwater's payroll lost a job to bank the first half-million dollars, and the order of the levers was the reason: Avoid through growth and attrition first, Cut on outside spend second, Grow third. Most organizations that do this well bank in that order, and they tell their people early.

Priya added one test to every Hour Budget before it could be signed. The budget owner, with the manager closest to the work, had to write the two sentences they'd say to the people whose work was changing. Tom Okafor wrote a pair for the inside-sales team: "We're not hiring the four reps in next year's plan, because this tool gives us that capacity. Nobody on this team is losing their job, and the time it frees goes first to the calls we've never had time to make." If a manager can't write those two sentences, the Hour Budget isn't ready.

And the best Hour Budgets do something else: they move experts back to expert work. Lena Ortiz, a senior credit analyst, had spent years doing clerical work that sat between Lena and the judgment Lena was hired for. Chapter 4 called that the eighth waste, unused judgment. Freeing Lena for the calls only an expert can make is one of the most valuable things an Hour Budget can do, and one of the hardest to price. Chapter 11 takes up Lena's story, and the whole conversation about people and jobs, in depth.

## Monday Morning

- Pick one live or nearly live automation and draft its Hour Budget on one page: every freed hour assigned to a lever, or written down as Leak.
- Open next year's headcount plan, contractor list and overtime report before you open any AI tool. That's where Cut and Avoid are hiding.
- Take the draft to the person whose budget line changes, and ask for their signature, a date and the evidence finance will accept.
- Look for one use case that shortens wait time in a money process and price it the Speed way: cash freed times your cost of capital.
- Write your two sentences for the people whose work is changing, and say them before the build starts.

*Freed hours become money only when someone with a budget decides, before the build, exactly where each one goes.*
