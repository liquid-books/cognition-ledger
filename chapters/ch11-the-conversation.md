## The Analyst Who Was Too Busy to Think

Lena Ortiz saw it coming three months before anyone else did.

She is a senior credit analyst at Brightwater Distribution, the fictional distributor we've followed through this book, and she has been reading customers for a living for most of her career. One Tuesday in spring, in the line for the break-room coffee machine, she mentioned something to Priya Shah almost in passing. A mid-sized contractor, a good customer for years, had changed the way it ordered. Smaller orders, more often, more rush shipping, and a new habit of paying the oldest invoices first and leaving the newer ones to age. "It looks like the pattern before a company runs out of cash," Lena said. "I keep meaning to write it up."

She never did. She didn't have time. Lena spent most of every week assembling credit memos for new accounts: pulling bank statements and trade references out of email, keying figures into the credit system, reformatting the same memo template, chasing a missing tax form. Thirty hours a week of that, by her own count, and about ten hours of the work she was actually hired for: judgment.

That summer the contractor stopped paying. Brightwater wrote off the balance. In the post-mortem, someone asked whether anybody had seen it coming, and Priya remembered the coffee line.

Here is what that story is really about. Brightwater's most valuable judgment had been sitting in the building the whole time, buried under its least valuable work. The company was paying an expert's salary for a clerk's week. Lean thinking has a name for this. It's the eighth waste, and in knowledge work it's the most expensive one: **unused judgment**, the waste of experts spending their week on clerical work.

When Sam Whitfield built the credit memo Skill (OTC-03, packaged expertise that drafts a new-account memo the Brightwater way, with a second review because the stakes are high), Lena became that second review. The Skill gathered, keyed and formatted. Lena read, questioned and decided. Her clerical hours fell to about four a week, review took about eight, and the rest of her week, close to thirty hours, went to the work only she could do: early-warning reviews of the largest accounts, visits to customers whose patterns worried her, and coaching two junior analysts.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody cut Lena's job. Nobody could have, without cutting the very judgment that made the Skill safe to use. That's the thread running through this whole chapter, and it's the conversation most AI programs avoid: what happens to people when the machines take over part of their work.

## The Question in Every Room

Walk into any company that has started using AI seriously and you'll find the same question in every room. It is rarely said out loud in meetings. It's said in the parking lot, in group chats, at kitchen tables. *Is my job going away?*

Most leaders handle that question the way people handle a strange noise from the car: they turn up the radio. They talk about productivity and "augmentation" and "freeing people for higher-value work," and they hope the question goes away. It doesn't. It goes underground, and underground is where the worst version of every story grows.

The Cognition Ledger won't let you dodge it, for a simple reason. The Hour Budget forces a decision for every freed hour, and some of those decisions are about people. The framework doesn't make the decision for you. It just refuses to let it stay vague.

You have two choices. You decide where the hours go, or the hours decide for you.

When the hours decide, one of two things happens. Either they drift, and Parkinson's law (work expands to fill the time available) swallows them, so the AI program quietly fails to bank anything and someone in finance eventually asks where the money is. Or, a year later, someone looks at a spreadsheet, sees a team "with spare capacity," and makes a panicked cut that nobody planned, nobody explained and nobody trained for. Both outcomes are bad for the company. The second is cruel to people. And both are what happens by default.

**Either you decide where the hours go, or the hours decide for you.**

History offers some comfort here, and an honest warning. When automated teller machines spread through American banks, plenty of people predicted the end of the bank teller. The economist James Bessen looked at what actually happened. Between 1988 and 2004 the number of tellers needed to run an average urban branch fell by about a third, because the machines took over cash handling. But cheaper branches meant banks opened more of them; urban branches grew by 43 percent. Total teller employment held roughly steady, and the job itself changed, from counting cash to building relationships and selling services. The machine took a task, not the job.

That's the comfort. Here is where the analogy breaks: an ATM did one task, and AI touches thousands of tasks across almost every office role at once, so there is no law of nature guaranteeing that every job survives in a new form. Economists have a name for the opposite mistake, the "lump of labour" fallacy, the belief that there is a fixed amount of work to go around, so every task a machine takes is a job lost forever. Both stories are too simple. What actually happens in your company depends on the decisions your leaders make, in what order, and how honestly they explain them. Which is exactly what the Hour Budget is for.

## Bank Before You Cut

There are six levers for freed hours. You met them in Chapter 8: Cut, Avoid, Grow, Fix, Speed and Invest. On paper they're equal. In practice, the organizations that bank AI time well, and keep their people's trust while they do it, pull them in a deliberate order.

**First, Avoid.** Absorb growth and natural attrition without the hires the plan called for. Nobody leaves. The hiring plan shrinks. This is the kindest lever and, conveniently, one of the easiest for finance to verify, because the evidence is an approved headcount plan with a requisition removed.

**Second, Cut external spend.** Stop paying for overtime, temporary staff, contractors and outsourced services. The spending leaves; your own people stay. Overtime, in particular, is usually the first place freed hours show up, and it's hard savings with a clean paper trail.

**Third, Grow.** Move hours to work that brings in revenue or keeps customers: proactive calls, account reviews, faster responses, new offers. People do more valuable work.

**Fourth, Fix, Speed and Invest.** Fewer errors and credits, faster cash, funded strategic projects. These levers create value without anyone needing to go anywhere. Some of them, you'll remember, don't even need hours: Brightwater's dispute agent freed \$3.45 million of cash simply by closing disputes three days sooner.

**Only then, fewer roles.** Sometimes the work truly shrinks and the people can't all move. When that happens, it should be a decision made openly, with notice, retraining offers and fair terms. A last resort, never a hidden default.

Brightwater's quote Skill shows the order at work. OTC-01 freed 10,246 hours a year. The Hour Budget sent 7,520 of them to Avoid: four inside-sales hires in the growth plan were never made, because Tom Okafor's existing team could now absorb the growth. That line is worth \$322,382 a year, priced at the loaded rate of the hires not made. Another 2,000 hours went to Grow: Tom's team now spends them calling lapsed accounts, priced at \$70,000 a year of gross margin. The last 726 hours are still waiting for a decision, and the ledger shows them as leak, at zero.

That's ten thousand hours banked, and nobody lost a job.

Cut works the same way, one step removed. At Brightwater, the collections team had brought in temporary staff every quarter-end for years, because the month-end crush of aging reports and reminder emails was more than the permanent team could carry. When the collections email Skill took over the drafting, the first thing the Hour Budget cut was the temp contract, not a person on the payroll. The evidence was a canceled purchase order, the kind of paper a CFO likes best. Nobody on the permanent team lost anything except a miserable week at the end of every quarter.

Avoid works because most organizations are not static. People retire, move, change careers. To turn that into a plan, the people partner in the AI Value Office (Chapter 10's fifth chair) builds a simple workforce plan for each value stream: how many people in each role, how many usually leave each year, how many the growth plan says to hire, and how many hours the ledger expects to free. Take a hypothetical team of 40 with 12 percent annual turnover. About five people leave in an ordinary year. If the Hour Budget frees the equivalent of five roles' worth of hours over the next eighteen months, a hiring pause on those backfills banks the money without a single layoff, provided you plan it in advance and don't discover it by accident. Avoid only works if someone writes it down before the requisitions open.

And if, after working down all five stairs, some roles truly must go? Then say so plainly and do it decently. Offer redeployment into the reviewer, exception and customer-facing work before anything else. Give real notice. Pay fair terms. Above all, don't disguise it as something else, because the people who stay will judge every future AI announcement by how you handled this one. A company that handles its first hard decision honestly earns the trust it needs for the next ten. A company that hides it has just taught everyone to hide their know-how from the next pilot.

Now the reveal, and it's the one that changes how executives think about AI and headcount.

The instinct is to cut the most expensive people first, because they free the most money per head. But look back at the time formula from Chapter 6: the new minutes per unit **include the time a person spends reviewing the AI's output**. Who does that review? Not the newest hire. It's done by people like Lena, people with enough experience to spot a wrong figure, a misread contract, a credit risk the model missed. The quality numbers in your ledger, the error rate and the cost of poor quality it avoids, depend on those reviewers. Cut them to bank their salaries and the error rate climbs, the cost of poor quality turns negative, and the savings eat themselves.

**The person who checks the machine has to know more than the machine.**

That makes expert judgment the one asset an AI program can't afford to lose. Which brings us to the conversation itself.

## Tell Them Early

Brightwater's CEO, Dana Ruiz, held a town hall before the first Skill went live. Her people partner had pushed hard for it, and her lawyers had pushed back, worried that saying anything would be read as a promise. In the end she said four things, and only things she could keep.

She said that the company was using AI to take over parts of the work in Order-to-Cash, starting with quotes and credit memos. She said that nobody would lose their job because of the Order-to-Cash Skills and agents in this year's plan, and that some roles the company had planned to hire would not be hired. She said that many people's work would change, and that the company would train them for the new work on company time. And she said when she would update everyone next, and she put the date on the screen.

Afterwards, Tom Okafor held his own version for the inside-sales team, and it was shorter. He put the OTC-01 Hour Budget on the wall and walked them through it line by line. Four hires that won't be made. Two thousand hours of calls to customers who had drifted away, which his team had complained for years they never had time to make. Seven hundred and twenty-six hours that nobody had decided on yet, and he asked the team for ideas. Somebody suggested a weekly review of lost quotes to learn why customers said no. It went into the ledger as a new idea at Intake that afternoon.

Notice what she did not say. She did not promise that no job would ever change or end. She did not use the word "augmentation." She did not pretend to know things she didn't. She told the truth she had, with a date for the truth she didn't have yet.

Every person in that room was carrying five questions, whether or not they asked them. Is my job going away? What will I do instead? Who decides? What will I learn? When will I hear more? A good leader answers all five, early and plainly, even when some of the answers are "not yet, and here's when."

Why early? Because silence is not neutral. When leaders say nothing, people fill the gap with the scariest version of the story, and they act on it. Your best people, the ones with options, start taking recruiters' calls. The people who stay start protecting their work instead of improving it: they hide shortcuts, keep know-how in their heads, and quietly make sure the AI pilot looks worse than it is. That last one matters more than most executives realize. Every Skill is somebody's know-how written down. If the somebody won't help, you get a worse Skill.

**Silence doesn't protect people. It hands the message to whoever tells the worst story.**

A practical rule for the message: never announce an AI tool without announcing the Hour Budget that goes with it. "We're rolling out a credit memo Skill" invites fear. "We're rolling out a credit memo Skill, the hours go to early-warning reviews of our top accounts, and Lena is leading that work" invites curiosity. The Hour Budget isn't just a finance document. It's the most reassuring thing you can show an anxious team, because it proves someone has thought about where the time goes.

## Protect the Judgment

Come back to Lena's two junior analysts. Before the Skill, they learned the job the way juniors in every profession have always learned it: by doing the routine cases. Gathering a hundred bank statements teaches you what a healthy one looks like. Keying a thousand trade references teaches you which ones smell wrong. The clerical work was never only clerical. It was the apprenticeship.

Now the Skill does the routine cases. That's the point of it. But here is the trap no AI vendor will warn you about: if you automate the apprentice work and stop there, the juniors never form the judgment that makes a senior analyst. Ten years from now, Lena retires, and the company discovers that nobody left in the building can tell when the credit Skill is wrong. The machine is still running. The expertise that checked it is gone.

The answer is to redesign the ladder before you remove its bottom rung. At Brightwater, juniors now learn by reviewing the Skill's drafts, side by side with Lena, and explaining every change they make. Reviewing a machine's work, it turns out, is a fast way to learn, because every draft is a worked example and every correction is a lesson. Analysts take the exceptions the Skill flags as unusual, which are exactly the cases that build judgment. And experts like Lena get a new job that didn't exist before: they write the Skills. A Skill is a folder of instructions, examples and reference files that captures how the house does a piece of thinking work. Somebody has to decide what goes in it. That somebody should be the best person you have.

This gives you the shape of reskilling in an AI-shaped company, and it's more concrete than most training catalogues. People move toward four kinds of work. **Reviewers** check the machine's output and own its quality. **Exception handlers** take the cases the machine routes to a human because they're unusual, risky or new. **Skill authors** turn expert know-how into packaged expertise that everyone can use. And **customer-facing roles** take the hours that Grow sends their way: the calls, visits and conversations a machine can prepare for but shouldn't hold alone. Each path should have a name, a start date and time set aside on the company's clock, because training squeezed into evenings is a promise people can see through.

Reskilling also belongs in the ledger, not just in HR's training calendar. Many Grow and Invest lines depend on redeployed people becoming good at new work. Tom's 2,000 hours of calls to lapsed accounts only bank if the people making those calls know how to win a customer back, and that is a skill, not a schedule change. So the AI Value Office treats training as part of the build: a Grow line isn't ready to bank until the people behind it have been trained, and the realization date allows for the months it takes them to get good. Leaders who skip this step blame the AI when the Grow line underdelivers. The AI did its part. The company didn't do its own.

Whatever we call these tools in ten years, the pattern will hold. Machines will keep getting better at the routine, and the routine will keep being how humans learn. The companies that thrive will be the ones that designed a new way to learn on purpose, rather than discovering a decade later that their experts had quietly stopped being made.

There's a paradox in asking experts to write Skills, and you should name it before they do. Writing down what you know can feel like writing yourself out of a job. If your best person's know-how becomes a folder anyone can use, what is your best person for? Experts who ask that question aren't being difficult. They're being rational, and the answer has to be rational too. Make Skill authorship a visible, credited part of the job, in performance reviews and in pay. Put the author's name in the Skill. Give experts the first claim on the freed hours, so the reward for writing down what they know is more time for the work they love, not less security. The companies that get this right find that experts compete to write Skills, because it's the clearest proof of their value the organization has ever offered them.

Lena, by the way, wrote her first Skill last quarter: an early-warning checklist that reads a customer's ordering and payment patterns and flags the ones that look like the contractor she spotted in the coffee line. Her name is in the description. The juniors call it "Lena's nose."

## Monday Morning

Before your next AI tool goes live, write the Hour Budget in the order Avoid, Cut external spend, Grow, then Fix, Speed and Invest, and treat fewer roles as a last resort that is decided openly. Ask your people partner for a one-page workforce plan for each value stream: roles, normal turnover, planned hires and expected freed hours. Name the experts whose judgment reviews your AI, and protect them by name. Redesign how juniors learn before you automate their routine work. Then schedule the conversation, answer the five questions, and put the date of your next update on the screen.

*AI changes the work; leaders decide what that means for people, and the only bad decision is the one nobody makes out loud.*
